# We Are Born Remembering, But We Learn to Forget — Script

A WebXR piece for Quest, phone and desktop (about 8–10 minutes).

**Premise.** A newborn still remembers its past lives. In each past life you first watch
the myth from outside, then become the figure who dies. Holding a hand and letting it go
runs through every life.

**Through-lines**
- **Poem:** Alfred, Lord Tennyson, *In Memoriam A.H.H.*, canto XXVII (1850). The scenes
  show it one line at a time; the prologue and finale show the whole stanza.
- **Music:** Beethoven (*Moonlight Sonata*, Symphonies 7 and 9, *Für Elise*).
- **Look:** cut-outs from public-domain paintings stand in an engraved, hand-coloured
  3D world, like a paper theatre. Myth quotations unroll on parchment scrolls.

**Translations quoted:** Samuel Butler (*Iliad* 1898, *Odyssey* 1900), H. T. Riley
(Ovid, *Metamorphoses*, 1851), Theodore C. Williams (Virgil, *Aeneid*), Benjamin
Jowett (Plato, *Republic*), and J. G. Frazer (Apollodorus, *Library*, 1921). All are public domain.

**Flow**
1. Title, then the Sphinx (onboarding), then Garden, then Crib (birth).
2. Icarus, then Crib (hearing).
3. Sirens, then Orpheus, then Beethoven.
4. Medusa, then Persephone's Garden, then Hades, then the Choice.
5. Holding On *or* Letting Go.
6. Afterlife, then Every Life at Once, then begin again or Credits.

On-screen narration is shown in *italics*. Myth quotations are shown as quotes, with their source.

## 0b · The Sphinx (the threshold, and the onboarding)
**Myth:** the riddle of the Sphinx (Apollodorus, *Library* III, trans. J. G. Frazer). Its answer, a human being on four feet, then two, then three, is the life you are about to live.
**What happens:** you stand in Moreau's gorge at dusk; the painting itself, its figures lifted out, wraps all around you. On the rock ahead the Sphinx clings to Oedipus beside the white-and-blue column and its urn. She teaches you to play: find a light off to your right, then hold your gaze on it (or click, tap, pull the trigger). Then she asks her riddle, and three lights answer: *a god*, *a beast*, *a human being*. A wrong light goes out; the right one blooms. She throws herself from the rock, and the light carries you into the Garden.
**Paintings:**
- Gustave Moreau, *Oedipus and the Sphinx* (1864, The Met): the Sphinx and Oedipus cut out; the gorge around you; its fig tree; the column and urn rebuilt in 3D.

**Text:**
- *At the gate of every life, the Sphinx is waiting.*
- *Look around — drag, move your phone, or turn your head.* / *Find the light.* / *Hold your gaze on it — or click, tap, pull the trigger.*
- *Every soul must answer her before it is born.*
- > "What is that which has one voice and yet becomes four-footed and two-footed and three-footed?" — *Library* III, Apollodorus
- *Answer her.* (wrong: *No. Look again.*)
- > "As a babe he is four-footed, going on four limbs, as an adult he is two-footed, and as an old man he gets besides a third support in a staff."
- > "So the Sphinx threw herself from the citadel."

## 1 · The Garden (prologue)
**Myth:** the Muses, daughters of Mnemosyne (Memory), invoked before the story begins; then the birth of Venus.
**What happens:** before birth, your soul stands in Botticelli's night garden. The Graces dance, Zephyr chases Chloris, Cupid circles overhead and petals fall. The grove then opens onto the sea, and Venus is born on her shell. So are you.
**Paintings:**
- Sandro Botticelli, *Primavera* (c. 1480): the Graces, Mercury, Flora, Zephyr and Chloris, Venus, Cupid, the meadow and the grove.
- Sandro Botticelli, *The Birth of Venus* (c. 1485): Venus, Zephyr and Aura, the Hora, the sea.

**Text:**
> "O Muses, tell me — for you are goddesses and are in all places so that you see all things, while we know nothing but by report." — *Iliad* II, Homer

> I hold it true, whate'er befall;
> I feel it when I sorrow most;
> 'Tis better to have loved and lost
> Than never to have loved at all.
> — Tennyson, *In Memoriam A.H.H.*, 1850

Title in 3D: *We are born remembering, / but we learn to forget.*

## 2 · The Crib (birth)
**Myth:** Hector, Andromache and their baby Astyanax (*Iliad* VI). The baby is frightened by his father's plumed helmet.
**What happens:** you are the baby in a Greek nursery, with your parents leaning over the crib. You try to speak. Each try breaks apart into a cry, and the adults only hush and pat you. Then a beam of light from the window finds your face and becomes the sun.
**Paintings:**
- J. H. W. Tischbein, *Hector's Farewell to Andromache* (1812): Hector, and Andromache's face.
- Carl Friedrich Deckler, *The Farewell of Hector*: the bronze helmet.
- Francesco Hayez, *Hector Greets Andromache and Astyanax*: hung on the wall, and the view of Troy.
- Benjamin West, *The Fright of Astyanax* (1766).
- Kleophrades Painter, Panathenaic amphora (c. 500 BC).

**Text:**
- *You remember everything. / You know exactly what to say.*
- *Try to speak.* The baby's attempts:
  - *I remember how I died —*
  - *Listen. I was falling —*
  - *I held a hand. It let go —*
  - *Please, I have to tell you —*
  - *I have lived before —*
- *But all that comes out is a cry.*
- > "The boy cried and nestled in his nurse's bosom, scared at the sight of his father's armour, and at the horse-hair plume that nodded fiercely from his helmet. His father and mother laughed to see him." — *Iliad* VI
- *The light from the window finds your face.*

## 3 · Icarus
**Myth:** Daedalus and Icarus (Ovid, *Metamorphoses* VIII).
**What happens:** you watch from the sky as father and son fly over an engraved Aegean, past islands, a lighthouse and fishermen who take them for gods, with clouds in layers below and around you. Icarus climbs toward the sun; when you look at the sun it swells, hums, and he climbs faster. Look at him and you become him: each beat of your arms takes you higher, the wax runs, the feathers leave your arms, his father calls, and you fall through the clouds.
**Interactions:** catch the feathers that fall past you (the ones you catch end up on your arms); part the clouds beside you; look at the sun and hurry him; beat your wings. On a flat screen: click a feather, click a cloud, look with the mouse, click to beat.
**Paintings:**
- Jacob Peter Gowy after Rubens, *The Fall of Icarus* (c. 1636): Daedalus, with the wings Gowy painted, and Icarus.
- *The Fall of Icarus*, engraving (1730, Rijksmuseum): the fishing boat and the rock, scissored out with a strip of bare paper.

**Text:**
- > "Icarus, I recommend thee to keep the middle tract; if thou shouldst go too low, the water should clog thy wings; if too high, the fire of the sun should scorch them." — *Metamorphoses* VIII
- > "A person while he is angling for fish, or the shepherd leaning on his crook, when he beholds them, is astonished, and believes them to be Divinities."
- > "Icarus, where art thou? In what spot shall I seek thee?"
- Poem line: *'Tis better to have loved and lost*

## 2b · The Crib (hearing)
**What happens:** you wake from the fall, crying. Then comes a sound behind the door: muffled thumps you can almost see. Looking toward it leads to the Sirens.
**Text:** *Another life. Another ending.* … *Something, behind the wall…*

---

## 4 · The Sirens
**Myth:** Ulysses and the Sirens (*Odyssey* XII). In Hyginus, the Sirens were fated to die once a ship sailed past them unharmed.
**What happens:** at dusk you float among the Sirens' rocks. Two Sirens climb from the sea, and bird-Sirens circle overhead. A Greek ship rows past with Ulysses bound to the mast and the crew's ears stopped with wax. The song does not hold him. You become a Siren: your voice fails, and you fall into the sea and turn to stone.
**Paintings:**
- Herbert James Draper, *Ulysses and the Sirens* (c. 1909): the Sirens climbing, and the Siren on the rope.
- John William Waterhouse, *Ulysses and the Sirens* (1891): the bird-Sirens and Ulysses at the mast.

**Text:**
- > "Come here, renowned Ulysses, honour to the Achaean name, and listen to our two voices. No one ever sailed past us without staying to hear the enchanting sweetness of our song." — *Odyssey* XII
- > "It was foretold that the Sirens would live only until a mortal heard their song and sailed on. When Ulysses passed, they threw themselves into the sea." — after Hyginus, *Fabulae* (our own wording)

---

## 5 · Orpheus and Eurydice
**Myth:** Orpheus leads Eurydice out of the underworld but must not look back (Ovid, *Metamorphoses* X).
**What happens:** you walk in a misty grove of the dead, behind Orpheus, who leads Eurydice toward the light playing the lullaby your mother hummed. He may not look back, and he can feel your eyes on them: hold him with your gaze and the lyre falters, and he turns. Their embrace is that instant. You become Eurydice: you are drawn back into the dark, you reach out, your hands close on air, and he grows small in the light. In that light the Muses appear, and Orpheus becomes Beethoven.
**Interactions:** walk behind them (they wait for you, for a while); pluck the lyre on the stone (the mist thins, the trees lean, the mourners turn and come closer, and he holds on a little longer); look at them, or look away; reach for him.
**Paintings:**
- Camille Corot, *Orpheus Leading Eurydice from the Underworld* (1861): the grove all around you, its leaves in the crowns of the trees, its undergrowth, its mourners.
- Peter Paul Rubens, *Orpheus and Eurydice* (1636–38): the two figures.
- Frederic Leighton, *Orpheus and Eurydice* (1864): the embrace.
- Rudolf Hausleithner, *Beethoven's Vision* (1882): the Muses, and Beethoven on his rock.

**Text:**
- > "The hero receives her, and, at the same time, this condition, that he turn not back his eyes until he has passed the Avernian valleys, or else that the grant will be revoked." — *Metamorphoses* X
- > "The ascending path is mounted in deep silence, steep, dark, and enveloped in deepening gloom."
- > "He, enamoured, fearing lest she should flag, and impatient to behold her, turned his eyes; and immediately she sank back again."
- > "And now, dying a second time, she did not at all complain of her husband; for why should she complain of being beloved? And now she pronounced the last farewell."

## 6 · Beethoven
**Myth:** Orpheus becomes Beethoven, a modern re-reading. The lyre becomes the piano, and the Muses return.
**What happens:** at night you are Beethoven, deaf, at an engraved mahogany grand piano. You play, but you hear nothing. Gold sand on the lid jumps and settles into Chladni figures, so you see the music you cannot hear. Then, all at once, you hear it: the Ninth Symphony floods the room. The sand rises as gold dust, the piano strings lift into Orpheus' lyre of light, and the Muses step out of the painting on the wall. A loved one's hand comes to rest on yours.
**Paintings:**
- Rudolf Hausleithner, *Beethoven's Vision* (1882): framed on the wall.
- The Muses from the same painting, stepping out of the frame.

**Text:**
- *Silence. You have not heard a sound in years.*
- *Play.*
- *You feel the music in the wood, in your bones.*
- *And then — you hear it.*
- *A hand holds yours.*
- Poem line: *I feel it when I sorrow most*

---

## 7 · Medusa
**Myth:** Perseus and Medusa (Ovid, *Metamorphoses* IV). In Ovid, Perseus beheads her while looking only at her reflection. Having her meet her own gaze is our retelling.
**What happens:** you stand in the Gorgons' grotto at the edge of the world. Around you are marble statues of the figures from your earlier lives: the Graces, Mercury, Icarus, a Siren and Orpheus. A polished shield comes out of the dark. In its bronze is Medusa's face, and it is yours. You turn to stone.
**Paintings:**
- Caravaggio, *Medusa* (c. 1597): the face in the shield.
- Edward Burne-Jones, *The Baleful Head* (1885): hung on the rock like an altarpiece.
- Cut-outs from the earlier scenes, re-tinted as marble statues.

**Text:**
- > "He saw everywhere, along the fields and the roads, statues of men and wild beasts turned into stone from their natural form, at the sight of Medusa." — *Metamorphoses* IV
- > "From the reflection on the brass of the shield which his left hand bore, he beheld the visage of the horrible Medusa."
- > "In the polished bronze she saw a face she had never seen. It was her own. And she, too, became stone." — a retelling

## 7b · Persephone's Garden
**Myth:** Persephone and Hades (*Homeric Hymn to Demeter*).
**What happens:** a garden you can walk in. Trees answer your hand: they shake, shed petals, sometimes let a fruit fall. Pomegranates can be picked, held and thrown. Then the earth opens in front of you and the chariot rises and takes her, while you watch. The light goes. A pomegranate glows on the great tree. Bring one to your mouth (or burst one on the ground) and you are her, and the ground lets you down.
**Interactions:** shake a tree; pick, hold and throw a fruit; taste it. On a flat screen: click a tree, click a fruit, click again to throw, E or the button to taste.
**Paintings:**
- Walter Crane, *The Fate of Persephone* (1877): the chariot, the maidens, the narcissi and the turf underfoot, the far orchard.
- Sandro Botticelli, *Primavera* (c. 1480): every leaf of the grove was lifted out one by one and laid into the crowns; the oranges were ripened to pomegranates. Bark from its trunks.

**Text:**
- *A garden with no winter in it. You have been here before.*
- > "She was playing with the deep-bosomed daughters of Oceanus and gathering flowers over a soft meadow." — *Hymn to Demeter*
- > "The wide-pathed earth yawned there in the plain of Nysa."
- *The flowers are still open. No one is there.*
- > "He secretly gave her sweet pomegranate seed to eat."
- *One seed. And now you are her.*

## 7c · Hades (the pop-up book)
**Myth:** Persephone in the underworld (*Homeric Hymn to Demeter*); Charon and the Styx.
**What happens:** a giant book lies shut on a dark floor. It opens at your feet and layers of cut paper stand up from the page. You can walk on it. Flames lean away from you and the shades step aside: you are still warm. Pull a paper tab and something hidden comes out; take the gold ribbon (or the page's corner) and pull to turn the page.
- Page 1: the Styx in layers, from Doré; the Rubens plate printed at your feet. Tab: the ferryman comes across.
- Page 2: the asphodel fields and the shades. Tab: Cerberus.
- Page 3: Rossetti's Proserpine under a paper arch. Tab: the king, behind her, in black paper.
- Page 4: the paper turns pale and the light comes down: Demeter, and Persephone led back up by Hermes.

**Paintings:**
- Gustave Doré, Charon, plate 10 of Dante's *Inferno* (1861): cut into the sky, hill, crowd, bank and water layers.
- Peter Paul Rubens, *The Rape of Proserpina* (1636–38): printed on the page.
- William Blake, *Cerberus* (1824–27).
- Dante Gabriel Rossetti, *Proserpine* (1874).
- Frederic Leighton, *The Return of Persephone* (1891).

**Text:**
- *The dead keep their stories in a book. It opens for you.*
- *The ferryman has never once looked back.*
- *They step away from you. You are still warm.*
- > "This realm rings with the triple-throated baying of vast Cerberus, couched huge in the cavern opposite." — Virgil, *Aeneid* VI
- *This is where she sits, half of every year.*
- > "He secretly gave her sweet pomegranate seed to eat." — *Hymn to Demeter*
- > "When the earth shall bloom with the fragrant flowers of spring in every kind, then from the realm of darkness and gloom thou shalt come up once more."
- *And you forget this, too.*

## 8 · The Choice
**Myth:** Achilles' two fates (*Iliad* IX). His visual restyle is still pending.
**What happens:** you are back in the crib, and your mother offers her hand. You can hold it or let go.
**Text:**
- *Your mother reaches for your hand.*
- *You know what it means to hold a hand.*
- *Every hand you held, you watched let go.*
- Choices: **hold her hand** / **let go**.
- Planned quotation: > "My mother Thetis tells me that there are two ways in which I may meet my end." — *Iliad* IX

## 9A · Holding On
**Myth:** Odysseus meets his mother's shade in Hades and tries three times to embrace her (*Odyssey* XI).
**What happens:** years pass in her hand. Then, one night in a Greek bedchamber, she dies. Her shade rises in light. You reach for her three times, and three times she slips from your arms.
**Paintings:**
- Jacques-Louis David, study for *Andromache Mourning Hector*: hung on the wall.
- Andromache's face, from Tischbein.

**Text:**
- *You hold on. / Years pass in her hand. / And then, as always, you watch her go.*
- > "My longing to know what you were doing, and the force of my affection for you — this it was that was the death of me." — *Odyssey* XI
- > "Thrice I sprang towards her and tried to clasp her in my arms, but each time she flitted from my embrace as it were a dream or phantom."
- *And you remember everything.*
- Poem line: *I hold it true, whate'er befall*

## 9B · Letting Go
**Myth:** Argos, the old dog who waited twenty years and was the only one to know Ulysses when he came home (*Odyssey* XVII).
**What happens:** you live a long life alone. Old, you sit in a loggia on Ithaca over the sea at dusk, with Argos at your feet. He knows you, then he dies. The sun goes down, and you close your eyes.
**Paintings:**
- Edwin Landseer, *The Old Shepherd's Chief Mourner* (1837): the dog, Argos.
- Theodoor van Thulden after Primaticcio, *Ulysses Known by Argos* (1633): the engraving on the wall.

**Text:**
- *You let go. / Nothing can be taken from you now.*
- > "As soon as he saw Ulysses standing there, he dropped his ears and wagged his tail, but he could not get close up to his master." — *Odyssey* XVII
- > "Argos died as soon as he had recognised his master."
- *The years pass quietly.*
- Poem line: *Than never to have loved at all.*
- *You close your eyes.*

## 10 · The Afterlife
**Myths:**
- Charon and the Styx, and Elysium (Virgil, *Aeneid* VI).
- The meadow of asphodel (*Odyssey* XXIV).
- Lethe and rebirth (Plato, *Republic* X, the Myth of Er).

**What happens:** you cross the Styx in Charon's boat and pay the obol. You pass the Isle of the Dead in the mist, while Cerberus bays from the far bank. Shades drift in the asphodel meadow. In Elysium, Orpheus still plays. At the river Lethe you cup the water, drink and forget, and are born again.
**Paintings:**
- Joachim Patinir, *Crossing the River Styx* (c. 1520): the river world.
- Arnold Böcklin, *Isle of the Dead* (1883).
- William Blake, *Cerberus* (1824–27).
- Carlos Schwabe, *The Elysian Fields* (1903): the meadow, and Orpheus with his lyre.

**Text:**
- > "Charon, the dread ferryman, guards these flowing streams, ragged and awful, his chin covered with untrimmed masses of hoary hair, and his glassy eyes aflame." — *Aeneid* VI
- > "This realm rings with the triple-throated baying of vast Cerberus, couched huge in the cavern opposite."
- > "They reached the meadow of asphodel where dwell the souls and shadows of them that can labour no more." — *Odyssey* XXIV
- > "They came to the happy place, the green pleasances and blissful seats of the Fortunate Woodlands. Here an ampler air clothes the meadows in lustrous sheen, and they know their own sun and a starlight of their own." — *Aeneid* VI
- > "With them is the Thracian priest in sweeping robe, and makes music to their measures."
- > "Souls, for whom second bodies are destined and due, drink at the wave of the Lethean stream the heedless water of long forgetfulness."
- > "They rested at evening by the river Unmindful, whose water could not be retained in any vessel; and he who drank forgot all things." — *Republic* X
- > "And suddenly they were all driven divers ways, shooting like stars to their birth."

## 11 · Every Life at Once (finale)
**Myth:** Glaucus on the generations of men (*Iliad* VI).
**What happens:** a montage cuts through every past life, each one a gilded oval on old paper, while olive and laurel leaves fall through all of them. The last oval matches your ending: Andromache's face if you held on, Argos if you let go. Then everything goes white, a newborn cries, and the whole poem appears once more.
**Paintings:** one image from each life:
- Botticelli's Venus
- Hayez's Hector and Andromache
- Gowy's Icarus
- Draper's Sirens
- Leighton's Orpheus and Eurydice
- Hausleithner's Beethoven
- Caravaggio's Medusa
- Schwabe's Elysium

**Text:**
- Flashes: *the sun · the sea · the silence · the music · her hand*
- > "Men come and go as leaves year by year upon the trees. Those of autumn the wind sheds upon the ground, but when spring returns the forest buds forth with fresh vines." — *Iliad* VI
- *And you are born again — remembering.*
- The full Tennyson stanza.
- Choices: **begin again** / **credits**
